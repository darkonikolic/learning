# How you talk to yourself when things go wrong

## What this is

When you make a mistake, fail at something, or go through a hard time, there is an automatic inner voice that responds. For most people under stress, that voice is harsher than anything they would say to another person.

Self-compassion is not about lowering your standards or pretending mistakes did not happen. It is about responding to your own struggles the same way you would respond to a good friend going through the same thing — with honesty, but without cruelty.

The research is clear on this: people who are hard on themselves do not perform better. They burn out faster, recover slower, and are more likely to avoid situations where they might fail again. Self-compassion is not softness — it is a more effective operating system.

There are three parts to it:
1. **Acknowledging what happened** — not minimising it, not dramatising it
2. **Recognising that struggle is part of being human** — you are not uniquely broken, everyone goes through hard things
3. **Responding with kindness** — not flattery, just basic decency toward yourself

## When to use this

- After you made a mistake and the inner voice will not stop
- When you are ashamed of something you did or did not do
- When you are holding yourself to a standard you would never apply to someone you care about
- When self-criticism is so loud it is stopping you from moving forward
- When you feel like you are fundamentally worse than other people

## How to do it

**The core practice — the friend question:**

When the self-critical voice gets loud, ask yourself one question:

> "If a good friend came to me and told me exactly what I just did — made the same mistake, said the same thing, failed in the same way — what would I say to them?"

Write it down or say it out loud. Then ask: "Why can I not say that to myself?"

This is not a trick. Most people immediately know what they would say to a friend. The gap between how they would treat a friend and how they treat themselves is where the work is.

---

**Three-step response after a mistake:**

**Step 1 — Name what happened, without spin.**
One sentence. Not "I completely destroyed everything" and not "it was nothing." What actually happened.
> "I lost my temper in that conversation."

**Step 2 — Normalise it.**
This is not about excusing it. It is about recognising that you are a human being under pressure, not a unique moral failure.
> "A lot of people struggle with this. I am going through something genuinely hard. This makes sense given what I am carrying."

**Step 3 — Ask what a compassionate response looks like.**
Not what punishment you deserve. What would actually help you do better next time.
> "What do I need right now? What would help me move forward?"

---

**The harsh voice audit:**

Once a week, write down the three harshest things you said to yourself. Then next to each one, write what you would say to a friend in the same situation.

Notice the difference. That difference is the practice.

---

**When you catch yourself mid-self-attack:**

Say internally: "This is a hard moment. It is okay that this is hard."

That is it. You do not need to fix the emotion or argue with the self-critical voice. Just acknowledge that what you are going through is genuinely difficult.

## Example

You said something you regret during a difficult exchange. The inner voice is running: "I am always like this. I cannot control myself. I am going to lose everything because of how I am."

**Step 1:** "I said something that came out badly and I regret it."

**Step 2:** "I am exhausted and under a lot of pressure. People say things they regret when they are in this state. That does not mean I am fundamentally broken."

**Step 3:** "What would actually help here? I need to apologise if necessary, understand what triggered me, and rest."

Compare to what you would say to a friend: "You are going through a lot. You slipped up. Apologise if you need to and then let it go. This one moment does not define you."

Now say that to yourself.

## What this is not

- It is not telling yourself that the mistake was fine when it was not
- It is not avoiding accountability
- It is not pretending you are happy when you are not
- It is not toxic positivity ("everything happens for a reason")

It is just basic human decency — applied to yourself.

# Six response types — and how to handle stacked accusations

## What this is

Under pressure, your brain wants to improvise. It mashes together moralising, defending, explaining, and counter-attacking all at once. The result is usually a mess that makes things worse.

Instead, learn to recognise which type of response the situation actually calls for, pick that one, and use only that one. There are six types. Each has a specific job.

## The six response types

| # | When to use it |
|---|----------------|
| 1 | The question is narrow and factual — just answer it directly |
| 2 | The question involves someone else's behaviour or wellbeing — redirect toward what you can do, especially around care for children or dependents |
| 3 | The question is vague or too broad — ask for precision before answering |
| 4 | You only know part of the answer — confirm what you know, say nothing beyond that |
| 5 | You are not the right person or channel for this — say so and redirect |
| 6 | The question contains a false or harmful assumption — refuse to accept the premise |

**The rule:** use one technique per response. Do not stack them. When you are anxious, the temptation is to use all six at once. That is the mistake.

## How to handle stacked accusations

When someone layers multiple accusations on top of each other — especially in formal or emotionally charged situations — use this three-step sequence:

**Step 1 — Refuse the premise:**
Do not accept the accusation as a starting point. Name that you are not accepting it.
Example: "That framing does not reflect what actually happened."

**Step 2 — State your boundary:**
Say clearly what you will and will not address.
Example: "I'm not going to respond to characterisations I don't recognise. What I can speak to is what I did."

**Step 3 — Redirect forward:**
Move toward something concrete and constructive.
Example: "What matters now is how we handle the next step."

Keep this short. The whole thing should take under fifteen seconds. Deliver it calmly, without matching the tone of the accusation.

## When to use this

- You receive a question and feel the urge to answer with everything at once
- Someone is stacking multiple attacks or accusations in one message
- You are in a formal or evaluative setting where improvising is risky
- You have just been hit with something provocative and need a structured way to respond

## Example

Accusation: "You've always been impossible to deal with, you ignored every message, and now you expect everyone to trust you?"

Step 1 — Refuse premise: "I'm not going to accept that summary."
Step 2 — Boundary: "I'll speak to my actual actions, not a generalisation."
Step 3 — Forward: "What I've done since [date] is [specific behaviour]. That's what I can account for."

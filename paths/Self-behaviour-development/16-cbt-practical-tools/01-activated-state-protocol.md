# Activated state protocol — when something hits you

## What this is and why it works

When something activates you — a message, a comment, a situation, a conversation — your brain shifts into defensive mode. In that state you do not think clearly, you do not assess accurately, and everything that comes out of you in the next few minutes is likely coming from fear or anger, not from genuine reasoning.

The problem is not that you get activated. The problem is that in an activated state you make decisions, send messages, and form conclusions about yourself and the situation that are not accurate — and that stay with you long after the moment has passed.

CBT (cognitive behavioural therapy) has a straightforward mechanism for this: **between the event and the reaction, insert a structured step that slows down and examines the thought before it becomes a belief or an action.** You are not eliminating the emotion — you are examining it.

This is not for every day. This is for moments when you notice you are activated and a thought is circling or pulling at you.

---

## When to use this protocol

Use it when you recognise at least one of the following:
- You received a message or comment that hit you and you have a strong impulse to respond immediately
- One thought about yourself or the situation keeps returning and you cannot release it
- You feel strong shame, anger, fear, or emptiness connected to a specific situation
- Something that happened an hour, a day, or a week ago is still "working" in you

---

## Protocol — three steps

### Step 1 — Immediately (0–2 minutes)

Put down the phone or close the laptop. Do not respond to anything.

Set a timer for **20 minutes**.

During this time: do not re-read the message, do not talk about the situation, do not analyse. Just wait. This is not weakness — it is technique. The brain in acute activation cannot examine objectively. 20 minutes changes the chemistry enough for the thought to become accessible.

If it is urgent and you must do something: write one neutral sentence that contains no position. "I see your message, I'll respond shortly." That is all.

---

### Step 2 — After 20 minutes (10–15 minutes)

Open this file. Answer the questions in order, in writing — not in your head. Writing slows down and concretises a thought in a way that thinking alone cannot.

---

**Question 1: What exactly happened?**

Write only the event — no interpretation, no "because", no "which means". One or two sentences.

> Example: "I received a message saying I was not present for my child last week."

> Not this: "I received a false accusation designed to paint me as a bad parent." — this is already interpretation, not event.

Your answer: ________

---

**Question 2: What was my first automatic thought?**

Write it literally, exactly as it came — even if it seems irrational or shameful. It is there. Better to see it than to push it down.

> Example: "Maybe they're right. Maybe I really am not enough."

> Example: "They're doing this deliberately to destroy me in court."

> Example: "I cannot take this anymore."

Your automatic thought: ________

---

**Question 3: How strong is the emotion? (0–10)**

Name the emotion as precisely as you can and give it a number.

"I feel bad" is not enough — bad is too broad. The mind does not know what to do with it.

Emotions common in these situations: **fear** (something bad will happen), **anger** (injustice, attack), **shame** (maybe they're right about me), **sadness** (loss, helplessness), **anxiety** (uncertainty, what will happen next).

> Example: "Shame — 8/10. Some anger — 5/10."

Your emotion and number: ________

---

**Question 4: Does this thought speak about the event, or about who I am?**

This is the key question. There are two types of automatic thoughts:

**Type A — thought about the situation:** "This message is untrue." / "This is a pressure tactic."
→ May be accurate, may not be — but it speaks about the event, not about you.

**Type B — thought about identity:** "I am not enough." / "I always ruin things." / "I am a bad parent."
→ This is an installed narrative that the event has activated. It is not the same as assessing the event.

If your automatic thought is Type B — it deserves specific attention in the following questions.

Your answer (Type A / Type B / both): ________

---

**Question 5: What is the evidence FOR this thought? What is the evidence AGAINST?**

This is the heart of the CBT approach. You are not asking "is this true?" — you are asking "what is the actual evidence?"

Fill both columns. If you skip one side — the exercise does not work.

| Evidence FOR this thought | Evidence AGAINST this thought |
|---|---|
| ________ | ________ |
| ________ | ________ |
| ________ | ________ |

> Example for the thought "I am not enough as a parent":
>
> | FOR | AGAINST |
> |---|---|
> | I didn't call my child for two days last week | Every week I have a routine with my child that has worked for years |
> | I sometimes raise my voice | I attended every school event this year |
> | We haven't talked about the problems they're having at school | My child comes to me when something is difficult |

Important: evidence must be concrete and verifiable — not feelings ("I feel like a bad parent") but actual events and behaviours.

---

**Question 6: What would a person who respects me say, having seen the same evidence?**

Not someone who flatters you. Not someone who attacks you. Someone with genuine goodwill who would be honest.

What would they say about your automatic thought?

> Example: "They would say that a few lapses don't define the whole picture. That the effort is visible. That the perfect parent is a myth."

Your answer: ________

---

**Question 7: What is the balanced thought — the one that includes both sides?**

Not optimistic. Not pessimistic. Accurate, as much as you can be.

> Example: "I have lapses as a parent, and I also have things I do well and consistently. One message does not describe the whole."

Your balanced thought: ________

---

**Question 8: How strong is the emotion now? (0–10)**

Same scale as before — what is the number now?

Emotion and number: ________

If it dropped by at least 2–3 points: the protocol worked. The emotion has not disappeared — it has shifted into something you can function from.

If it has not dropped: that is normal. Sometimes a second pass is needed, or the thought is more complex and warrants work with a therapist. It is not failure — it is information.

---

### Step 3 — Decision about response

Now, from this state, decide:

- Does this need a response? (Not always necessary)
- If yes — what is one sentence grounded in facts, not in emotion?
- If no — what do I do instead? (Use [Reset protocol](../14-psychology-engineering/02-reset-protocol.md) or [Observer log](../03-observer-loop/02-observer-daily-log.md))

## Three worked examples

### Example 1 — Message with accusation

**Situation:** Received a message: "The child tells me that when you're with them you're always on your phone and not paying attention."

**Automatic thought:** "Maybe I really am like that. Maybe I don't see how much I neglect my child."

**Emotion before:** Shame 7/10, anxiety 6/10.

**Type of thought:** Type B — speaks about identity.

| Evidence FOR | Evidence AGAINST |
|---|---|
| I sometimes check my phone when we're together | Every weekend we do an activity my child chooses |
| I once cancelled an outing because of work | My child tells me what's happening at school every day |
| — | When my child asks for attention, I put my phone away |

**What someone who respects me would say:** "Every parent checks their phone sometimes. The question is overall presence, not perfection. The overall picture is not neglect."

**Balanced thought:** "I have moments when I am not fully present, and I also have many moments when I am. This message describes one aspect, not the whole."

**Emotion after:** Shame 3/10, anxiety 3/10.

**Decision:** Will not respond to the message today. Next week I will consciously keep my phone in my pocket for the entire time with my child.

---

### Example 2 — Thought circling before sleep

**Situation:** No external trigger — but the thought "I'm not good enough" is circling before sleep.

**Automatic thought:** "Everyone else is functioning better. I am barely surviving."

**Emotion before:** Sadness 8/10, listlessness 6/10.

**Type of thought:** Type B — identity + comparison.

| Evidence FOR | Evidence AGAINST |
|---|---|
| Last week I didn't finish an important task | This month I managed to take my child to every scheduled appointment |
| I feel exhausted most days | I have not missed a single work deadline in the past three months |
| — | I have been through a period that would exhaust anyone |

**What someone who respects me would say:** "You are functioning under pressure that most people could not sustain. 'Barely surviving' in these circumstances is actually surviving — which is not nothing."

**Balanced thought:** "I am exhausted and it is affecting my functioning. I am still functioning. This is not a state that describes who I am — it describes the current pressure."

**Emotion after:** Sadness 4/10, listlessness 3/10.

**Decision:** No action tonight. Tomorrow morning — one item from the morning protocol.

---

### Example 3 — After a difficult formal meeting

**Situation:** A meeting with a lawyer / mediator / institution where you were under pressure and did not say everything you intended.

**Automatic thought:** "I showed myself badly again. I'm not capable of defending myself when it matters."

**Emotion before:** Anger (at self) 7/10, frustration 8/10.

**Type of thought:** Type B — capability + identity.

| Evidence FOR | Evidence AGAINST |
|---|---|
| I didn't say X that I had planned | I stayed calm throughout the entire meeting |
| I accepted one premise I shouldn't have | I asked one important question that changed the direction |
| I forgot a key document | I arrived prepared with material |

**What someone who respects me would say:** "You entered a difficult meeting under pressure. Not everything went perfectly and you didn't sink either. One poor showing does not define capability."

**Balanced thought:** "This meeting did not go as I wanted. I have things to improve. That is concrete information for next time — not evidence about who I am."

**Emotion after:** Anger 3/10, frustration 4/10.

**Decision:** Write notes on what I would do differently — for preparation before the next meeting. Not rumination.

---

## Most common mistakes when using this

**Mistake 1: Using the protocol in the hot moment**
It does not work. The brain is not accessible. Wait at least 20 minutes.

**Mistake 2: Skipping the evidence AGAINST column**
"There is nothing AGAINST" — almost never true. If you cannot find anything, that is a signal you are still too activated, not that there is no evidence.

**Mistake 3: Writing feelings instead of evidence**
"I feel like a bad parent" is not evidence. "I missed the agreed appointment on Tuesday" is evidence.

**Mistake 4: A balanced thought that is just optimistic**
"Actually I'm great and everything is fine" — that is not a balanced thought, that is a defence. A balanced thought includes what did not go well.

# Recurring thought protocol — when the same thought will not stop

## What this is and why it differs from the activated state protocol

There are two kinds of difficult thoughts:

**Kind 1 — Reactive thought:** something happened, you are not present, your brain reacts. For that there is the [Activated state protocol](./01-activated-state-protocol.md).

**Kind 2 — Recurring thought:** no concrete trigger, but the same thought keeps returning. Before sleep. In the shower. While driving. In the silence between situations. No new event — but the thought is always there, slowly draining energy.

Recurring thoughts are more dangerous than reactive ones because they have no clear trigger to signal that you should address them. They work quietly, in the background, and gradually shape how you see yourself and your situation — almost without you noticing.

This protocol is for that second kind.

---

## How to recognise a recurring thought

Ask yourself:
- Does this thought come every day, or multiple times a week, without a specific cause?
- Do you already sense how the thought begins the moment you have a little silence?
- Do you try to shake it off or stay busy to avoid feeling it?
- Has this thought existed for months or years?

Two or more yes answers — this is a recurring thought and it warrants this protocol.

---

## Protocol — five steps

### Step 1 — Name the thought precisely (5 minutes)

Recurring thoughts are often vague — "something is wrong with me", "this will not end well", "I am not good enough". That vagueness gives them power, because you cannot examine something that has not been precisely formulated.

Write the thought as a concrete statement. Do not paraphrase — write it literally, as it arrives.

> Vague (not this): "I have a feeling that something is off with me."
>
> Precise (this): "I believe I am fundamentally a bad person and that others have seen this."

> Vague: "I worry about the future."
>
> Precise: "I believe the court will rule against me and I will lose contact with my child."

> Vague: "I feel worthless."
>
> Precise: "I believe I am not worthy of love and that everyone around me would be better off without me."

Your precise thought: ________

---

### Step 2 — Classify the thought (3 minutes)

Once you have the precise formulation, ask yourself:

**Is this thought a prediction, an assessment, or an identity statement?**

| Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Prediction** | About what will happen in the future | "The court will rule against me." |
| **Assessment** | About what something means or its value | "What I did is unacceptable." |
| **Identity statement** | About who I am as a person | "I am a bad parent / bad person / a failure." |

Why classification matters:
- Predictions can be tested against reality — what do the actual facts say about the future?
- Assessments can be nuanced — one behaviour ≠ total worth
- Identity statements are the most vulnerable to installed narratives — they almost always call for an audit from [file 01 of phase 15](../15-identity-and-narrative-reconstruction/01-inherited-narrative-audit.md)

Type of your thought: ________

---

### Step 3 — Examine the thought (10–15 minutes)

The questions differ depending on the type of thought.

---

#### If it is a PREDICTION:

**Q1: What is this prediction based on?**
List concrete pieces of evidence that support it — actual events only, not feelings.

________

**Q2: What evidence speaks against it or reduces its probability?**

________

**Q3: How many times in the past have you predicted a similar outcome and been right? How many times wrong?**

Our minds are biased prediction machines — we systematically overestimate negative outcomes. This question asks you to check your own prediction track record.

________

**Q4: If the prediction comes true — what would you do? Have you survived difficult things before?**

This is not optimism. This is a realistic assessment of capacity.

________

**Q5: What is the more realistic version of this prediction, based on all the evidence?**

________

---

#### If it is an ASSESSMENT:

**Q1: What specifically am I assessing as unacceptable / bad / a failure?**
Write the specific event or behaviour — not the general picture.

________

**Q2: Does this single behaviour or event describe the whole?**

The mind under distress makes generalisations — one event becomes "always", "never", "everyone", "nothing". Write what would be a more accurate formulation.

> Instead of: "I wasn't present for my child."
> More accurate: "On that Tuesday I was not as present as I wanted to be."

________

**Q3: What standard would you apply to another person in the same situation?**

We are almost always harder on ourselves than on others. Write how you would assess another person in an identical situation with identical circumstances.

________

**Q4: What would be a fair assessment — one that includes context and the whole picture?**

________

---

#### If it is an IDENTITY STATEMENT:

This is the most complex type. Before examining the thought here — run the [Inherited narrative audit](../15-identity-and-narrative-reconstruction/01-inherited-narrative-audit.md) to establish whether this statement is yours or installed.

If you have already done the audit, or if you are uncertain, continue:

**Q1: Where does this statement come from? When did it first appear?**

________

**Q2: What concrete evidence from your actual behaviour supports this statement?**

Literal examples. Not "I feel bad" — but "I did X."

________

**Q3: What concrete evidence from your actual behaviour contradicts or complicates it?**

Literal examples. This is the hardest question. Do not skip it.

________

**Q4: If this statement were entirely true — how do you explain the evidence from Q3?**

A mind holding an identity statement must ignore contradictions. Here you bring them into awareness.

________

**Q5: What would be a more accurate identity statement — one grounded in all the evidence from both Q2 and Q3?**

> Example: Instead of "I am a bad parent" → "I am a parent who has lapses and who tries and who is consistently present. No parent is either perfect or simply bad."

________

---

### Step 4 — Define the new thought and anchor it (5 minutes)

Write one sentence that represents a more accurate version of the original recurring thought — grounded in the evidence you have just reviewed.

This is not an affirmation. It is not "everything is fine." It is a thought you have earned through examination.

> Example of original thought: "I am fundamentally a bad person."
> New thought: "I have flaws and I have qualities. This thought comes from a period of pressure, not from an objective assessment."

> Example of original thought: "The court will rule against me."
> New thought: "I do not know what the outcome will be. What I can control is preparation and conduct."

Your new thought: ________

**Anchor it:** Write it on paper and put it somewhere visible, or save it in your phone as a reminder. The recurring thought will not stop immediately — but each time it arrives, you can attach the new thought to it rather than swallowing it whole.

---

### Step 5 — Track over time

A recurring thought that has existed for years will not disappear after one pass of this protocol. But it weakens with each pass.

Log:

```
Date:                     ________
Original thought:         ________
Type (prediction/assessment/identity): ________
New thought:              ________
Strength before (0–10):   ________
Strength after (0–10):    ________
Note:                     ________
```

Once a week: look at entries for the same thought type. Is the number trending downward over time? If not — the thought is more complex and is worth exploring with a CBT-trained therapist.

## Four worked examples

### Example 1 — Identity statement (parenting)

**Original thought:** "I am not enough as a parent and my child would be better off if I were less present in their life."

**Type:** Identity statement — installed through prolonged conflict.

**Q1 (origin):** This thought intensified during the separation. I did not have it before that.

**Q2 (evidence FOR):**
- I sometimes raise my voice
- I was physically absent some weeks due to work

**Q3 (evidence AGAINST):**
- Every week I have a routine with my child that has functioned for years
- My child comes to me when they have a problem
- I have attended every important event
- My child seeks my company

**Q4 (explaining the contradiction):** A mind holding this thought must minimise everything in Q3 as "not enough" — but that standard would not be applied to any parent anywhere.

**Q5 (more accurate statement):** "I am a parent who has lapses and who is consistently present and to whom my child matters. This thought is not my assessment — it was installed through a conflict situation."

**Strength before:** 8/10. **After:** 4/10.

---

### Example 2 — Prediction (outcome of proceedings)

**Original thought:** "I will lose the case and lose contact with my child."

**Type:** Prediction.

**Q1 (evidence FOR):**
- The other side has more resources
- One decision has already gone against me

**Q2 (evidence AGAINST):**
- I have documentation and witnesses
- The previous negative decision was procedural, not on the merits
- Courts generally do not exclude a parent without strong grounds

**Q3 (prediction track record):** Over the past year I predicted five "certain" bad outcomes. Two partially came true, three did not.

**Q4 (if it comes true):** I would be devastated. And — there are legal appeal pathways. I am not without options. I have survived worse than I thought I could.

**Q5 (realistic prediction):** "I do not know what the outcome will be. I have reason to believe I am not in an insignificant position. Preparation is what I can control."

**Strength before:** 9/10. **After:** 5/10.

---

### Example 3 — Assessment (behaviour in conflict)

**Original thought:** "What I said during that argument is unacceptable and proves I am not capable of normal relationships."

**Type:** Assessment.

**Q1 (concrete event):** In one argument three months ago I said something harsh and unfair.

**Q2 (the generalisation):** One argument → "incapable of normal relationships" is a leap that does not follow logically.

**Q3 (standard for another person):** If a friend told me they had once said something harsh in an argument, I would not conclude they were "incapable of normal relationships." I would say they were under pressure and this was an isolated event.

**Q4 (fairer assessment):** "I said something harsh in one argument under significant pressure. That is behaviour not to repeat — it is not a characterisation of my entire capacity for relationships."

**Strength before:** 7/10. **After:** 3/10.

---

### Example 4 — Combined (worth and future)

**Original thought:** "Nobody truly loves me and I will end up alone."

**Type:** Identity statement + prediction. Break it into parts.

*Part A — "nobody truly loves me":*
Evidence FOR: Some relationships have cooled or ended during this difficult period.
Evidence AGAINST: I have friends who have been there. My child wants my presence. A colleague reached out out of concern.
More accurate thought: "Some relationships have cooled. I also have relationships where care is shown. 'Nobody' is not accurate."

*Part B — "I will end up alone":*
This is a prediction based on current state, not on an inevitable future.
More accurate thought: "I currently feel isolated. That is the state of this period, not a permanent outcome."

**Combined new thought:** "I am going through a period of isolation that is a consequence of circumstances. That is not a permanent state nor proof of my worth."

**Strength before:** 9/10. **After:** 5/10.

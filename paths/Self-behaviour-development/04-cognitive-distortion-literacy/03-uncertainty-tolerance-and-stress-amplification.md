# Sitting with uncertainty — not forcing a resolution too soon

## What this is

When something is unresolved, the discomfort of not knowing is real. The urge to resolve it — to get an answer, to decide what it means, to send the message that will force a conclusion — is also real. But acting on that urge too quickly often creates a second problem on top of the original one.

This file is about two related things:

1. **Sitting with "I don't know yet"** without immediately forcing a resolution
2. **Stress amplification** — the way stress makes threats feel bigger and more urgent than they actually are, which makes the urge to resolve even harder to resist

## When to use this

- You are waiting for a response and interpreting the silence in the worst possible way
- You feel the urge to send a message that would force a resolution before you have enough information
- You keep returning to an unresolved situation and cannot leave it alone
- You know rationally that you should wait but cannot hold yourself back from acting

## How to do it

**Step 1 — Name your specific uncertainty triggers.**

List five situations where uncertainty is hardest for you personally. Examples: no reply for more than a day, not knowing how a formal process is going, ambiguous logistics around schedules. For each one, write one honest sentence you can actually say or think: "I'll answer once I've verified the details." The sentence needs to be true — a real commitment to a real timeline.

**Step 2 — Ninety-second urge surf.**

When the urge to act (send the message, force the conclusion, reopen the conflict) spikes:
- Set a timer for 90 seconds
- Do nothing during those 90 seconds
- Note whether the urgency actually changes

This is practice. The goal is not to eliminate the urge. The goal is to see that the urge rises and falls on its own without you acting on it. Do this twice a week on real situations.

**Step 3 — Night rule.**

If history shows that you read messages worse after a certain hour — set a cutoff. No conflict threads after that time. This is not avoidance. It is accurate scheduling: you are not better at resolving things at 11pm than at 9am, and the evidence in your message history probably shows that.

**Step 4 — Notice stress amplification in your body.**

Stress amplification has physical signals — heat in the face or chest, tunnel vision, a sense of urgency that feels physical. When you notice these, run the grounding reset (feet, jaw, slow breath out) before re-engaging with the uncertain situation. The urgency is partly your nervous system, not purely the facts of the situation.

**Step 5 — Protect recovery time deliberately.**

Prolonged uncertainty is exhausting. This is not a weakness — it is a cost. Schedule recovery blocks explicitly, especially during periods when multiple things are unresolved at once.

## Example

It is 10pm. You sent a message about a schedule change hours ago and have not heard back. You have now checked the message six times. You are composing a follow-up that will likely escalate things.

Ninety-second urge surf: set the timer, put the phone down. Notice the urgency. After 90 seconds, ask: do I have new information? No. Is sending a follow-up now likely to help? No. Set the phone down. Come back to it tomorrow morning.

# Three biases that distort your judgment under stress

## What this is

Beyond individual thinking errors, there are three deeper patterns that affect how your mind processes information when you are stressed or in conflict. They work quietly in the background, shaping what you notice, how you explain behaviour, and what conclusions feel obvious.

Naming them helps you catch them before they drive a decision or a message.

## The three patterns

**1. Blaming character instead of circumstances**
When someone does something that affects you negatively, the stressed mind jumps to: "that's just who they are" or "they did that because they are [hostile/selfish/dishonest]". It skips over the simpler explanation: they were under pressure, they misread the situation, they made a mistake.

You do not do this to yourself. When you behave badly, you explain it through circumstances: "I was exhausted", "I was under enormous pressure". Other people rarely get the same generous reading.

**2. Confirmation bias — only noticing what fits the verdict**
Once you have formed a view about a person or situation, your mind preferentially notices evidence that confirms it and discounts or forgets evidence that contradicts it. This is not deliberate. It happens automatically.

The practical consequence: if you believe someone is out to get you, every ambiguous action becomes more evidence. Every cooperative action gets minimised or reframed.

**3. Availability bias — the most vivid recent example feels like a universal rule**
The last difficult message, the last bad call, the last fight — these feel representative because they are fresh and emotionally charged. Your brain treats them as the whole picture, not one data point.

"They always do this" — usually based on two or three vivid recent examples, not an actual pattern.

## When to use this

- After a conflict, before deciding what it means long-term
- When you are about to send something that starts with "you always" or "this proves"
- When you notice you cannot find anything positive to say about a person or situation
- When your judgments consistently get worse at night or at the end of a heavy week (check sleep and load first)

## How to do it

**Internal headline vs camera caption:**
For a recent friction, write two versions:
- The internal headline — what your mind said happened ("they are deliberately making this difficult")
- The camera caption — what an outside observer would say without interpretation ("they cancelled at two hours notice")

If the gap between these is large, do not send anything built on the headline.

**Disconfirm search:**
Write one sentence of evidence against your current theory. It must be honest. If you genuinely cannot find any, write: "unknown — I have not looked for disconfirming evidence."

**Multiple possible motives:**
Instead of "they did it because X", write: "possible reasons include ___, ___, ___." Stop before choosing one. Unless you have actual evidence for one explanation, leave it plural.

**Stress and sleep check:**
If your bias labels are spiking at night or after a difficult week, read the sleep and load file first. The same situation often reads completely differently when you are rested.

## Example

A co-parent does not respond to a logistics message for 24 hours. Internal headline: "They're ignoring me to create problems." Camera caption: "24 hours without a response to a logistics message." Disconfirm search: "They responded quickly to the last three messages about scheduling."

Multiple motives: busy, phone issues, overwhelmed, didn't see it, waiting for more information. Leave it there until there is actual evidence.

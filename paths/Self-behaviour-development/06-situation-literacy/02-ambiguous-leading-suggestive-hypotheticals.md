# Questions with hidden assumptions — how to handle them

## What this is

Some questions are not really questions. They contain a built-in assumption, and if you answer the question as asked, you have already accepted the assumption. This is how leading questions work.

The classic example: "When did you stop being angry all the time?" The question assumes you were angry all the time. If you answer it — even to deny it — you are debating on those terms. The right move is to identify the hidden assumption and refuse to accept it before you say anything else.

The same applies to hypothetical scenarios: "If it turned out you had done X, wouldn't you have to admit that Y?" You did not agree to the premise. You do not owe an answer to a hypothetical you never accepted.

## When to use this

- A question feels like it has already decided something about you
- You notice the question assumes something that is not true
- Someone asks you to respond to a hypothetical scenario as if it were established fact
- You feel pressure to defend yourself against a framing you do not recognise

## How to do it

**Step 1 — Identify the hidden assumption.**
Before you answer, ask yourself: what is this question taking for granted? Say it internally: "This question assumes that..."

**Step 2 — Narrow the question down.**
Instead of accepting the framing and defending yourself within it, bring the conversation back to specifics.
Useful phrases:
- "Which specific instance are you referring to?"
- "What timeframe are you asking about?"
- "Can you be more precise about what you mean by that?"

Narrowing is not evasion. It is refusing to debate something that has not been established.

**Step 3 — For hypotheticals, decline to build the scenario.**
You do not have to construct an answer to an imaginary situation you never agreed to. A clean response: "I'm not going to speculate about a scenario I don't recognise. What actually happened is..."

**Step 4 — Watch your recordings.**
If you are practising, review what you said. The thing to catch is any sentence that starts with "yeah, I guess if that were true..." — that is you accepting the premise. That sentence should not be there.

## Example

Question: "Why do you always make everything about yourself?"

Hidden assumption: that you always do this.

Wrong response: "I don't always do that, sometimes I'm very considerate and here are three examples..."
(You accepted the framing and are now defending yourself inside it.)

Right response: "I don't recognise that as a pattern. Is there something specific you want to talk about?"

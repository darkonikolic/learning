# What stability actually looks like — behaviours others can observe

## What this is

Stability is not a feeling. It is not something you claim. It is a set of behaviours that other people can see and verify over time. Evaluators, judges, and anyone watching you in a caregiving role will judge stability by what you do consistently — not by what you say about yourself.

This file helps you identify those behaviours, rate yourself honestly on them, and pick one specific thing to improve this week.

## What counts as observable stability

These are the kinds of behaviours that register with outside observers:

- Arriving on time — to pickups, appointments, school events
- Keeping children out of adult conflict — not discussing the other parent negatively in front of them, not asking them to carry messages
- Maintaining a calm tone under stress — especially in exchanges with the other parent or in formal settings
- Consistent routines — bedtimes, meals, school preparation, homework
- Following through on what you say you will do
- Responding to communications promptly and without escalation
- Keeping formal exchanges brief, factual, and free of blame

## How to do it

**Step 1 — Rate yourself honestly.**
Go through the list above (or build your own version). For each behaviour, give yourself an honest score:
- 0 = I am not doing this at all
- 1 = I do this sometimes, inconsistently
- 2 = I do this most of the time
- 3 = I do this reliably

Be honest. No one is reading this except you, and inflation here only hurts you.

**Step 2 — Pick one behaviour to improve this week.**
Not three. Not everything at once. One. The one where a small, consistent improvement would be most visible or most meaningful.

**Step 3 — Define what "better" looks like.**
Make it specific. Not "I'll be calmer" but "When I receive a message that provokes me, I will wait 20 minutes before responding." Concrete and testable.

**Step 4 — Check next week.**
Did you do the thing you said? If yes, pick the next one. If no, investigate why, not as self-criticism, but as information.

## Spoken version (for evaluative settings)

If you are asked to describe what you provide as a caregiver, keep it practical and short. One minute or less. Describe what the child actually receives from you: predictability, a calm environment, consistency. Mention one real, specific moment if it helps. Then stop.

No melodrama. No "I would do anything for my child." Just what you actually do, described plainly.

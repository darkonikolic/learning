# Building your own narrative — a story from evidence, not from hope

> Your own story is not built from affirmations you don't believe. It is built slowly, from concrete evidence out of real experience, while the installed narrative gradually loses ground.

---

## Why affirmations don't work (when someone has convinced you that you are bad)

If someone tells you "you're a great parent" and you deeply believe you're not — your mind rejects it. It is too far from the installed belief.

What works is something more modest and precise: an **evidence log** — a record of concrete moments that align with who you actually are, without exaggeration.

Not "I am a great parent." But: "today I sat with my child for 20 minutes and was present. That happened."

The accumulation of those records, over time, becomes the foundation of a new narrative — grounded in real experience, not in wishful thinking.

---

## Part 1 — Definition: who you are by behaviour

A story about yourself cannot begin from feeling ("I feel like a good person") or from someone else's approval ("they say I'm..."). It begins from **behaviour that is visible and repeatable**.

Fill this in:

```
Three things I do consistently, without an audience:
1. ________
2. ________
3. ________

Three values I would recognise in my own behaviour — even in difficult periods:
1. ________
2. ________
3. ________

One thing I know to be true about myself, even when pressure pushes me to forget it:
________
```

These are starting points. They don't need to be grand — the more concrete they are, the more resistant they are to external pressure.

---

## Part 2 — Evidence log (daily practice, 2 minutes)

Every day, one sentence. Concrete, not emotional.

Format:
> "[Date] — [What you did / how you behaved] — this is consistent with [value]."

Examples:
> "12.03.2026 — Waited for my child to finish their sentence before responding. Patience."

> "14.03.2026 — Did not respond to a provocative message today. Chose not to engage. Self-control."

> "17.03.2026 — Acknowledged a mistake to the children directly and without drama. Accountability."

**Rules:**
- One entry — no more, no less
- Only what actually happened
- Do not write anything you are not certain is true
- Do not skip days when things were hard — those days especially have something worth recording

**Log format (weekly review):**

```
Week: ________

Monday:    ________
Tuesday:   ________
Wednesday: ________
Thursday:  ________
Friday:    ________
Saturday:  ________
Sunday:    ________

Which value appeared most often this week: ________
Which value was harder to show: ________
```

---

## Part 3 — Identity statement (not an affirmation — a claim from evidence)

After 2–3 weeks of the evidence log, write three sentences about yourself in first person.

Rules:
- Each must be supported by at least one concrete example from the log
- No superlatives ("I am the best")
- No direct refutation of someone else's story ("I am not what they say")
- Written about behaviour, not about feelings

Examples:
> "I am someone who remains present for my children even when exhausted. I showed this on [concrete moment]."

> "I make decisions from accountability, not from fear — even when that costs me in the short term. I showed this when [concrete moment]."

> "I know the difference between a mistake and a character defect. I make mistakes, correct them when I can, and move on."

Write your three:

```
1. ________
   Evidence: ________

2. ________
   Evidence: ________

3. ________
   Evidence: ________
```

These statements are your foundation when external pressure tries to push you back into the installed narrative.

---

## Part 4 — Protecting the narrative under pressure

The installed narrative returns most easily during:
- Physical exhaustion
- Unexpected attack (written, verbal, or official)
- Silence — when there is no external validation for an extended period
- After a mistake

For each of these moments, prepare one anchor — a short sentence you return to.

```
When exhausted, my anchor is:       ________
When under attack, my anchor is:    ________
When validation is absent, my anchor is: ________
When I make a mistake, my anchor is: ________
```

An anchor is not false comfort. An anchor is a sentence grounded in evidence from the log — something you know to be true even when you don't feel that it is.

---

## Part 5 — Separating from someone else's story in real time

When you notice that someone's words are activating an installed narrative (using the mindfulness practice from file 02):

1. **Pause** — do not respond immediately
2. **Name it**: "this is targeting an installed story about me"
3. **Anchor**: say your identity statement to yourself
4. **Classify the message**: I / P / H (from [Message classification drill](../06-situation-literacy/03-message-classification-drill.md))
5. **Respond from your narrative**, not from theirs

Example sequence:

> You receive a message: "You were never a stable parent."
>
> Pause. → "This is targeting the installed narrative." → Anchor: "I remain present for my children." → Classification: P (provocation) → Routing 6 (refuse premise) + Routing 2 (care-forward) → Respond with one factual sentence or do not respond.

---

## How this develops over time

```
Weeks 1–2:  Audit (file 01) — understand what is not yours
Weeks 3–4:  Mindfulness (file 02) — practise the gap between thought and reaction
Weeks 5+:   Evidence log + identity statements — build your own foundation
Month 2+:   Anchors become automatic, installed narrative loses force
```

This is not a fast process. The installed narrative was built over years — your own takes months. What matters is regularity, not speed.

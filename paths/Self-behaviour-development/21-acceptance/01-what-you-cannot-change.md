# Acceptance — what to do with things you cannot change

## What this is

There are two categories of difficult things in life:

1. **Things you can influence** — your own behaviour, your decisions, how you prepare, how you respond
2. **Things you cannot change** — other people's choices, the past, how a process unfolds, outcomes that are outside your control

The problem is that most people spend similar amounts of energy on both categories. Fighting something you cannot change feels like doing something about it. It is not. It is just suffering twice — once because the thing is hard, and again because you are resisting the fact that it is hard.

Acceptance is not resignation. It does not mean you give up or stop trying. It means you stop wasting energy fighting the fact that something is the way it is, and redirect that energy toward what you can actually do.

There is a useful distinction: **you can fully accept that something is happening and still take every available action in response to it.** These are not opposites.

## When to use this

- When you have been going around in circles about something you cannot change
- When you are exhausted from fighting a situation rather than responding to it
- When anger about the unfairness of something is consuming your energy without producing any progress
- When you are waiting for someone else to be different before you can feel okay
- When the question "why is this happening to me?" is running on a loop

## How to do it

### Step 1 — Sort what is in your control and what is not

Write down what is bothering you. Then draw a line and sort each element:

| What I can influence | What I cannot change |
|---|---|
| How I prepare | The other person's behaviour |
| My response in the moment | What already happened |
| What I choose to focus on | How the process unfolds |
| Who I ask for help | What other people think of me |

This is not a philosophical exercise — it is a practical sorting. The left column gets your action and attention. The right column gets acceptance.

---

### Step 2 — Name the resistance

When you are not accepting something, there is usually a specific story underneath it. Often one of these:

- "This should not be happening to me" — but it is
- "They should be different" — but they are not
- "This is unfair" — it may well be, and that does not change what is
- "If I accept this I am giving up" — acceptance is not giving up

Name which story is running. Naming it does not fix it immediately but it makes it visible, which means you can choose what to do with it rather than being driven by it.

---

### Step 3 — The acceptance statement

This is not positive thinking. It is a plain factual acknowledgement of what is true.

Format:
> "This is what is happening. I did not choose this and I do not like it. It is happening anyway."

Examples:
> "The process is slow and I cannot speed it up. I did not choose this and I do not like it. It is happening anyway."

> "The other person is behaving in a way I find unfair. I cannot make them behave differently. This is what is happening."

> "Things did not go the way I hoped. I cannot undo it. This is what happened."

Say it plainly. You are not trying to feel good about it. You are just stopping the fight with reality.

---

### Step 4 — Redirect to what you can do

After acceptance, there is usually action available. Not to change the unchangeable thing, but to respond to it effectively.

Ask: "Given that this is what is happening — what is one thing I can actually do?"

One thing. Not a complete solution. Just one concrete next step from a position of accepting reality rather than fighting it.

---

### The grief distinction

Sometimes what looks like a need for acceptance is actually grief — loss of what you hoped would happen, loss of how things used to be, loss of a relationship or situation. Grief is not the same as acceptance work, and it should not be rushed.

If something feels too raw to "accept" — if the idea of acceptance feels like betrayal or giving up something important — that may be grief that needs to move through first. That is different from resistance to what is. Give it room.

## Example

You are in a legal process and the pace is frustrating. You cannot speed it up. You have done everything you can do on your side. The waiting is consuming a significant portion of your mental energy every day.

**What is in your control:** preparation, your legal team, your conduct, what you do with your time now.

**What is not:** the pace of the process, the other party's behaviour, the timeline.

**Resistance story running:** "This should not be taking this long. They are doing this deliberately."

**Acceptance statement:** "The process is slow. I cannot change that. This is what is happening."

**Redirected action:** "What can I do today that is useful — not about this, just in my actual life?"

You will probably need to do this more than once. Acceptance is not a single decision. It is a repeated return, each time the resistance flares back up.

## What acceptance is not

- It is not agreeing that something is okay when it is not
- It is not pretending you are fine
- It is not giving up on things you can actually influence
- It is not a one-time achievement — it requires returning to it repeatedly
